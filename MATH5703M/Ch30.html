Lecture 30 Confidence Versus Credibility

This is the R session for week 10. In lectures this week we have introduced priors, posteriors and credible intervals. In this session we compute a confidence interval and a credible interval for the same data, and we look at how a prior turns into a posterior as observations are added. As before, appendix B contains a written reference for the R commands used.

30.1 The Same Data, Two Intervals

We take the data of example 26.9, T=37T=37 successes in n=100n=100 Bernoulli trials, and compute two intervals for the success probability θ\theta. The first is the Wald interval x¯±1.96⁢x¯⁢(1−x¯)/n\bar{x}\pm 1.96\sqrt{\bar{x}(1-\bar{x})/n} of that example. For the second we use the Bayesian model of lecture 28 with the flat prior: the posterior is Beta⁢(1+T, 1+n−T)\text{Beta}(1+T,\,1+n-T) by example 28.5, and the interval between its 2.5%2.5\% and 97.5%97.5\% quantiles is the equal-tailed credible interval with credibility 0.950.95 from definition 29.1. The command qbeta can be used to find these quantiles. Before you run the code, predict whether the credible interval will be wider or narrower than the Wald interval, and whether it will sit to the left or to the right of it.

n <- 100
total <- 37
xbar <- total / n
se <- sqrt(xbar * (1 - xbar) / n)
xbar + c(-1, 1) * qnorm(0.975) * se
a <- 1 + total
b <- 1 + n - total
qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), a, b)
## [1] 0.2753721 0.4646279
## [1] 0.2817646 0.4680688

The two intervals, [0.275,0.465][0.275,0.465] and [0.282,0.468][0.282,0.468], agree to within 0.010.01. The credible interval is slightly shifted towards 1/21/2, since the flat prior adds one success and one failure to the data. Although the numbers are close, the two statements are different. The Wald interval comes with the guarantee that the recipe used to compute it covers the truth in approximately 95%95\% of samples, and it makes no statement about this particular interval. The credible interval means that, given the observations and the flat prior, the probability that θ\theta lies between 0.2820.282 and 0.4680.468 is 0.950.95. Thus the sentence which was wrong for the confidence intervals of section 27.1 is the correct interpretation here, since θ\theta is a random variable in the Bayesian model. A different prior would give a different interval, and the numerical agreement is a property of the flat prior and of the sample size.

30.2 From Prior to Posterior

We now look at the update from example 28.5 one observation at a time. In the script R/S30-posterior.R we simulate 100100 Bernoulli observations with θ=0.3\theta=0.3. After kk observations with ss successes, the prior Beta⁢(a,b)\text{Beta}(a,b) has turned into the posterior Beta⁢(a+s,b+k−s)\text{Beta}(a+s,\,b+k-s), and we plot this posterior density for three priors: the flat prior Beta⁢(1,1)\text{Beta}(1,1), the Jeffreys prior Beta⁢(1/2,1/2)\text{Beta}(1/2,1/2) from example 28.10 and the misplaced prior Beta⁢(12,3)\text{Beta}(12,3), with mean 0.80.8 and equivalent sample size 1515. In the simulated sequence, the first five observations are all failures, there are 22 successes among the first 2020 and 2828 among all 100100. Before you look at figure 30.1, sketch the panel for k=5k=5: which curves are steep, where are their peaks and how wide are the curves?

At k=0k=0 the three curves in figure 30.1 are the priors, and we can see that the Jeffreys prior is not flat. At k=5k=5 the two weak posteriors take their largest values at 0, since no successes have been observed so far, while the misplaced posterior has only moved from 0.80.8 to around 0.60.6. At k=20k=20 all three posteriors are humps, narrower than before. The two weak posteriors sit well below the truth after an unlucky start, and the misplaced one is closest to 0.30.3; this shows how much the prior still matters after twenty observations, not that the misplaced prior is correct. At k=100k=100 the two weak posteriors coincide and the misplaced posterior overlaps them: as the weight of the prior decreases, as discussed in example 28.5, every posterior concentrates near the truth.

30.3 A Task to Take Home

For the data of section 30.1, compute the equal-tailed credible interval with credibility 0.950.95 under the prior Beta⁢(12,3)\text{Beta}(12,3) from section 30.2, and compare it with the two intervals found there. Then compute all three intervals for T=4T=4 successes in n=10n=10 trials. Write a short paragraph about how the influence of the prior depends on the sample size.

Lecture 19 The EM Algorithm

In lecture 5 we found the MLE by solving the likelihood equation, in closed form where this is possible and numerically otherwise. In this lecture we consider models with latent variables or missing data, where the likelihood would be easy to maximise if the missing values were known. The expectation-maximisation (EM) algorithm, introduced in this lecture, makes use of this structure: it alternates between filling in the missing information and re-estimating the parameter values. We illustrate the EM algorithm with the help of a worked example, considering a mixture of two normal distributions. This example also gives the non-identifiable model from lecture 4, to which we return in lecture 20. There we will also prove that each round of the EM algorithm cannot decrease the likelihood, and in lecture 21 we will discuss how to implement the EM algorithm in R.

19.1 Incomplete Data

Assume that we are given data consisting of i.i.d. pairs (Xi,Zi)(X_{i},Z_{i}), i=1,…,ni=1,\dots,n, with joint density or joint probability weights fc⁢(x,z;θ)f_{c}(x,z;\theta), but we have observed only X=(X1,…,Xn)X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}). The pair (X,Z)(X,Z) with Z=(Z1,…,Zn)Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}) is called the complete data, the values XX are called the observed data and the unobserved values ZiZ_{i} are called latent variables. The complete-data log-likelihood is given by

ℓc⁢(θ)=∑i=1nlog⁡fc⁢(Xi,Zi;θ),\ell_{c}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f_{c}(X_{i},Z_{i};\theta),

and the observed-data log-likelihood is given by

ℓ⁢(θ)=∑i=1nlog⁡f⁢(Xi;θ),\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(X_{i};\theta),

where ff is the marginal density of a single observation, given by

f⁢(x;θ)=∑zfc⁢(x,z;θ),f(x;\theta)=\sum_{z}f_{c}(x,z;\theta),

with the integral used instead of the sum when ZiZ_{i} is continuous. The MLE for θ\theta is the maximiser of ℓ\ell, but the algebra from lecture 5 is not applicable, since the sum inside the logarithm prevents any simplifications. The function ℓc\ell_{c}, being a sum of standard log-likelihoods, would be easy to maximise, but it cannot even be evaluated as it depends on ZZ. The EM algorithm is based on this observation.

We will use the following example throughout this lecture and the next.

Example 19.1.

Suppose that a population consists of two groups, with proportion π\pi for group 1 and 1−π1-\pi for group 2. The measurement has distribution N⁢(μ1,σ2)N(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2}) in group 1 and N⁢(μ2,σ2)N(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2}) in group 2, where we assume that we know σ2\sigma^{2}, while π\pi, μ1\mu_{1} and μ2\mu_{2} are unknown. We are given observations of the measurement XiX_{i}, but we do not know which group Zi∈{1,2}Z_{i}\in\{1,2\} the observation belongs to. If we write φσ\varphi_{\sigma} for the density of N⁢(0,σ2)N(0,\sigma^{2}), then the observations are i.i.d. with density

f⁢(x;θ)=π⁢φσ⁢(x−μ1)+(1−π)⁢φσ⁢(x−μ2),f(x;\theta)=\pi\,\varphi_{\sigma}(x-\mu_{1})+(1-\pi)\,\varphi_{\sigma}(x-\mu_{% 2}),

where the parameter is

θ=(π,μ1,μ2)∈(0,1)×ℝ×ℝ.\theta=(\pi,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\in(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.

This is an example of a two-component normal mixture. The observed-data log-likelihood is

ℓ⁢(θ)=∑i=1nlog⁡(π⁢φσ⁢(Xi−μ1)+(1−π)⁢φσ⁢(Xi−μ2)),\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\bigl{(}\pi\,\varphi_{\sigma}(X_{i}-\mu_{1})+(1% -\pi)\,\varphi_{\sigma}(X_{i}-\mu_{2})\bigr{)},

with a sum of two terms inside each logarithm. If we knew the groups, the MLEs for this example would be easy to find: we would get the proportion of group 1 and the two group means by using examples 5.3 and 5.4, but without the groups we cannot find the MLEs in closed form.

19.2 The Algorithm

The EM algorithm starts from a guess θ0\theta_{0} and then alternates between two steps: it replaces ℓc\ell_{c} by its conditional expectation given the observed data, computed under the current parameter value, and it maximises the result over θ\theta.

Definition 19.2.

Let θ0∈Θ\theta_{0}\in\Theta be given. Then the EM algorithm for finding the parameter values θ1,θ2,…\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots consists of the following iterative steps for k=0,1,2,…k=0,1,2,\dots:

  1. (E)
    ​

    Expectation step: compute

    Q⁢(θ|θk)=𝔼θk⁢(ℓc⁢(θ)|X),Q(\theta\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mu\theta_{k})=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{k}}\bigl{(}% \ell_{c}(\theta)\!\mathrel{\big{|}}\!X\bigr{)},

    the conditional expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood given the observed data, where the conditional distribution of ZZ is computed using the parameter value θk\theta_{k}.

  2. (M)
    ​

    Maximisation step: set

    θk+1=arg maxθ∈ΘQ⁢(θ|θk).\theta_{k+1}=\mathop{\textnormal{arg\,max}}_{\theta\in\Theta}Q(\theta\mskip 1.% 0mu|\mskip 1.0mu\theta_{k}).

The second argument θk\theta_{k} of QQ enters only through the conditional distribution of ZZ given XX. By Bayes’ rule, proposition A.20 in appendix A, this conditional distribution has density or probability weights

g⁢(z|x;θ)=fc⁢(x,z;θ)f⁢(x;θ).g(z\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mux;\theta)=\frac{f_{c}(x,z;\theta)}{f(x;\theta)}.

Since the pairs (Xi,Zi)(X_{i},Z_{i}) are independent, once the data are observed Q⁢(θ|θk)Q(\theta\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mu\theta_{k}) is a sum over the observations, given by

Q⁢(θ|θk)=∑i=1n∑zg⁢(z|xi;θk)⁢log⁡fc⁢(xi,z;θ).Q(\theta\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mu\theta_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{z}g(z\mskip 1% .0mu|\mskip 1.0mux_{i};\theta_{k})\,\log f_{c}(x_{i},z;\theta).

For a discrete latent variable the complete-data log-likelihood is linear in the indicators 𝟏{Zi=z}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{i}=z\}}, since

log⁡fc⁢(xi,Zi;θ)=∑z𝟏{Zi=z}⁢log⁡fc⁢(xi,z;θ).\log f_{c}(x_{i},Z_{i};\theta)=\sum_{z}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{i}=z\}}\,\log f_{c}(x_% {i},z;\theta).

Comparing this with the formula for Q⁢(θ|θk)Q(\theta\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mu\theta_{k}), we see that the E step replaces each indicator 𝟏{Zi=z}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{i}=z\}} by its conditional probability g⁢(z|xi;θk)g(z\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mux_{i};\theta_{k}) given the data.

We now apply the algorithm to the two-component normal mixture from example 19.1. We will see that the M step gives the complete-data MLEs from lecture 5, with each group indicator replaced by the conditional probability that the observation belongs to the group.

Example 19.1 (continued).

In this model, the latent variables Z1,…,ZnZ_{1},\dots,Z_{n} are i.i.d. with ℙ⁢(Zi=1)=π\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=1)=\pi and ℙ⁢(Zi=2)=1−π\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=2)=1-\pi, and given Zi=jZ_{i}=j we have Xi∼N⁢(μj,σ2)X_{i}\sim N(\mu_{j},\sigma^{2}). The complete-data density is fc⁢(x,1;θ)=π⁢φσ⁢(x−μ1)f_{c}(x,1;\theta)=\pi\varphi_{\sigma}(x-\mu_{1}) and fc⁢(x,2;θ)=(1−π)⁢φσ⁢(x−μ2)f_{c}(x,2;\theta)=(1-\pi)\varphi_{\sigma}(x-\mu_{2}), and thus the complete-data log-likelihood is

ℓc⁢(θ)=∑i=1n(𝟏{Zi=1}⁢(log⁡π+log⁡φσ⁢(xi−μ1))+𝟏{Zi=2}⁢(log⁡(1−π)+log⁡φσ⁢(xi−μ2))),\ell_{c}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl{(}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{i}=1\}}\bigl{(}\log\pi% +\log\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{i}-\mu_{1})\bigr{)}+\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{i}=2\}}\bigl{(}% \log(1-\pi)+\log\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{i}-\mu_{2})\bigr{)}\Bigr{)},

which is linear in the indicators. For the E step, using Bayes’ rule with the current value θk=(πk,μ1,k,μ2,k)\theta_{k}=(\pi_{k},\mu_{1,k},\mu_{2,k}), we find

γi,k=ℙθk⁢(Zi=1|Xi=xi)=πk⁢φσ⁢(xi−μ1,k)πk⁢φσ⁢(xi−μ1,k)+(1−πk)⁢φσ⁢(xi−μ2,k).\gamma_{i,k}=\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{k}}(Z_{i}=1\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0muX_{i}=x_{% i})=\frac{\pi_{k}\,\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{i}-\mu_{1,k})}{\pi_{k}\,\varphi_{\sigma% }(x_{i}-\mu_{1,k})+(1-\pi_{k})\,\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{i}-\mu_{2,k})}.

This is called the responsibility of group 1 for the observation ii. By this linearity, taking the conditional expectation replaces 𝟏{Zi=1}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{i}=1\}} by γi,k\gamma_{i,k} and 𝟏{Zi=2}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{i}=2\}} by 1−γi,k1-\gamma_{i,k}, and thus

Q⁢(θ|θk)=∑i=1n(γi,k⁢(log⁡π+log⁡φσ⁢(xi−μ1))+(1−γi,k)⁢(log⁡(1−π)+log⁡φσ⁢(xi−μ2))).Q(\theta\mskip 1.0mu|\mskip 1.0mu\theta_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl{(}\gamma_{i,k% }\bigl{(}\log\pi+\log\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{i}-\mu_{1})\bigr{)}+(1-\gamma_{i,k})% \bigl{(}\log(1-\pi)+\log\varphi_{\sigma}(x_{i}-\mu_{2})\bigr{)}\Bigr{)}.

For the M step we maximise over θ=(π,μ1,μ2)\theta=(\pi,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}) with the γi,k\gamma_{i,k} fixed. Since log⁡φσ⁢(x−μ)=−(x−μ)2/(2⁢σ2)+const\log\varphi_{\sigma}(x-\mu)=-(x-\mu)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})+\text{const}, the partial derivatives of QQ are

∂Q∂π\displaystyle\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\pi}
=∑iγi,kπ−∑i(1−γi,k)1−π,\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}\gamma_{i,k}}{\pi}-\frac{\sum_{i}(1-\gamma_{i,k})}% {1-\pi},

=1σ2⁢∑i=1nγi,k⁢(xi−μ1),∂Q∂μ2=1σ2⁢∑i=1n(1−γi,k)⁢(xi−μ2),\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,k}(x_{i}-\mu_{1}),% \qquad\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\mu_{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-% \gamma_{i,k})(x_{i}-\mu_{2}),

and setting these to zero gives the updates

equation (19.1) (19.1)
πk+1=1n⁢∑i=1nγi,k,μ1,k+1=∑iγi,k⁢xi∑iγi,k,μ2,k+1=∑i(1−γi,k)⁢xi∑i(1−γi,k).\pi_{k+1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i,k},\qquad\mu_{1,k+1}=\frac{\sum_{% i}\gamma_{i,k}x_{i}}{\sum_{i}\gamma_{i,k}},\qquad\mu_{2,k+1}=\frac{\sum_{i}(1-% \gamma_{i,k})x_{i}}{\sum_{i}(1-\gamma_{i,k})}.

The second derivatives are negative and thus these are maxima. They are the complete-data MLEs of examples 5.3 and 5.4 with indicators replaced by responsibilities.
